What should it take to ground an engine?

A missed failure is an engine that fails in service. A false alarm is an unnecessary inspection. They are not equally expensive — so the alert threshold is a real engineering decision, not a default. Drag it.

Precision vs recall across every threshold
The shipped model sits at 0.30, chosen on F2 (recall weighted twice precision). Leaving it at the 0.50 default would miss 74 failures instead of 39 — nearly double, to avoid 152 cheap inspections.

Three regressors, honestly scored

The gap is the story

Both tree models train to about 10.3–10.9 RMSE and validate near 16. That ~5-point spread is real overfitting, reported rather than hidden. The linear baseline shows no gap at all — it is high-bias, not high-variance.

Where it fails

The model regresses toward fleet-typical wear, so unusually fast-failing engines are reported healthier than they are — unit 41 has 18 cycles left and is predicted 43.6. That is the dangerous direction, which is why the recall-tuned classifier, not the RUL number, is the safer trigger.

How it works

Raw telemetry to risk call

STAGE 01

Load & label

20,631 engine-cycles. RUL for training is simply max cycle minus current cycle, per engine.

STAGE 02

Engineer

Rolling mean and std over 5 and 10 cycles, plus a 10-cycle degradation slope — strictly backward-looking, grouped per engine. 7 sensors sit at zero variance and are dropped.

STAGE 03

Split by engine

Never by row. Adjacent cycles of one engine on both sides of a split would leak its whole trajectory. Zero unit overlap is asserted in code.

STAGE 04

Train & select

XGBoost for RUL on last-cycle RMSE; Random Forest for risk on F2, because recall is worth more than precision here.

STAGE 05

Serve

FastAPI. Rejects histories under 10 cycles — below that the trend features collapse and a failing engine reads as healthy.
